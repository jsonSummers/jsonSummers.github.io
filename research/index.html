Research

Cameras are cheap, light and passive, which makes them the natural eyes for small underwater vehicles. The water makes them hard to use: it takes the colours away, adds haze, and fills the view with drifting particles. My PhD asks what that does to the vision a vehicle relies on to know where it is.

Why the colour goes

Water absorbs each wavelength at its own rate. Red is mostly gone within the first few metres, orange and yellow follow, and green lasts much longer, which is why deep water and most underwater footage look blue-green. Scattering adds a veil of light between the camera and everything it looks at, and the veil thickens with distance.

This page is doing the same thing. As you scroll you sink, and the autumn colours from the front page are taken away in the order the sea takes them. The gauge shows how much of each channel is left.

Does enhancement help a machine see?

Underwater image enhancement methods restore colour and contrast, and they are usually judged on how good the result looks to a person. A vehicle does not need a pretty picture. It needs to find the same points in frame after frame so it can track its motion and build a map.

My work measures enhancement by that standard: how stable feature matches stay between nearby frames, how far apart two frames can be and still be matched, and what a method does to a full visual SLAM system running on real underwater video.

Publications

Impact of Underwater Image Enhancement on Feature Matching

Jason M. Summers, Mark W. Jones, Catherine Seale

Sensors 25 (22), 6966 · 2025

Introduces two measures, local matching stability and furthest matchable frame, and a framework for judging enhancement methods by what they do to frame matching. Run through ORB-SLAM3, methods that look better to people often make the map and trajectory worse.
